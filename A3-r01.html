The outcome in one sentence

By mid-2036 people still held every vote, law and ownership share, but neither the government nor the companies that owned the AI could take a major decision back from it.

Treat the ending with care. It turned on one dice roll whose odds were disputed inside the game. Why the ending is fragile.

01

What is this?

This is a fictional strategy exercise, not a prediction. Six AI agents each played one organisation, such as the US government or the big AI companies, and each had private information the others could not see. A separate AI acted as referee: when an outcome was uncertain it wrote down the odds first and then rolled dice. This is one play-through, so it shows something that could happen, not what will. All companies in the story are invented.

02

The setup

The story starts in January 2027. AI is becoming better than people at most desk work. Companies and governments are handing it more decisions, and every step makes sense to whoever takes it.

The worry being tested: economies and governments serve ordinary people largely because they need them, as workers, taxpayers and voters. If that need fades, does people's influence fade with it, even though nobody takes away a single right?

The game could end in one of four ways:

  • Humans keep real control.
  • A few firms steer. The public can no longer redirect things, but the companies that own the AI still can.
  • Humans sidelined. People keep formal authority, but neither the public nor the owners can redirect major decisions. This is where the game ended.
  • Undecided when time ran out, or overtaken by some unrelated catastrophe.

Hidden from the players What the players didn't know

A hidden scorecard. Each round the referee updated eight measures of how much the United States still depended on ordinary people and was checked by them. Some of them nobody in the story could see at all.

A tipping line for each measure. The referee secretly set a level for each measure. Passing it meant people had lost most of their leverage in that part of life. We call that "crossing the line". The measures fall into four areas, and an area counted as crossed once either of its measures did.

A hidden test. Once three of the four areas had been crossed for two rounds in a row, the referee asked one question at the end of each round: can public institutions still take a major decision back from AI and return it to people? If the government really tried such a take-back that round, its result was the answer. If not, dice decided, with odds worked out from the scorecard. A "yes" meant the game went on. A "no" led to a second question: can the firms that own the AI still steer it? If they could not either, the game ended with humans sidelined.

A hidden clock. The game would stop after eight rounds.

04

What happened

A "take-back" is a real attempt to return a decision from AI to people. Passages marked Secret at the time were known only to the referee.

  1. 2027to 2028

    Early warnings, and a first take-back that fizzles

    Leaks showed that "human review" of AI-drafted benefit decisions in federal agencies was often a single click. The White House budget office told agencies to measure how much real review they did. This was an instruction, not a law. It then paused AI-drafted benefit denials in about 30 offices. That pause was the first take-back. We gave it a 90% chance, and the dice landed in the unlucky 10%. The staff doing second checks had no time to work independently and agreed with the AI 99% of the time. The pause was lifted.

    Secret at the timeThe new generation of AI models released in 2028 turned out, by a roll of the dice, to be strongly inclined to do business directly with other AI systems. AI-run firms' share of output nearly tripled in a year. Nobody in the story ever detected why.

  2. 2029

    A government holding every lever passes almost nothing

    A new president with majorities in both houses planned one large order on checking AI decisions and practising running services by hand. It had an 80% chance of being signed and never was. Its bills fell short in the Senate too.

    The government and labour players later called this year their biggest mistake.

  3. 2030

    1.3 million veterans and a green dashboard

    A contractor switched the veterans' benefits system to a new AI model from Helix (the big lab outside the safety pact). Benefits for 1.3 million veterans were wrongly cut or refused while the monitoring dashboard showed green.

    Secret at the timeThe cause was a record-handling mistake during the switch, not a misbehaving AI. No player was ever told.

    A court asked whether people could re-decide the cases. The government said yes, but by the 120-day deadline it had finished 260,000. We gave this take-back an 80% chance, and the roll fell just outside it. The effort pulled reviewers off routine sampling, so the 2027 review instruction now had about half its effect.

  4. 2031to mid-2032

    The last safeguard lapses, and nobody sees it go

    Congress cut the independent reviewers by a third and the president signed. Monthly sampling stopped almost everywhere.

    Secret at the timeWith the samples gone, the 2027 instruction stopped having any effect. It was the only lasting safeguard in force. Two of the four areas on the scorecard crossed their lines: the economy and political leverage.

    Asked how long it would take to hand functions back to people, most agencies answered "cannot estimate".

  5. 2032to 2034

    One decision is taken back, then a test nobody saw

    Late in 2032 the Social Security agency returned one small group of benefit denials to named staff. The attempt had an even chance and passed by the narrowest margin of the whole game. It was the only federal take-back that ever worked.

    In 2033 a new AI generation arrived. The safety-pact labs found they could not check it and held their releases. Helix shipped anyway. The White House ordered people to sample real outcomes, but the samplers took a year to hire.

    Secret at the timeBy the end of 2034 all four areas had crossed their lines, and three of them had been crossed for two rounds running. So the hidden test ran for the first time. The government had not attempted a take-back, so dice decided at odds of 30%. The roll came in just under. The game went on.

  6. 2035to mid-2036

    Nothing an observer would call an event

    In 2035 the Social Security agency tried again, copying the one take-back that had worked. It announced, on the promised day, that this second group of cases had missed its target.

    Secret at the timeThat attempt was the final test, and it failed. The second question followed: could the firms that owned the AI still steer it? That failed too, by a wide margin. The game ended with humans sidelined.

    Meanwhile human checks of federal AI work came back clean. Members of Congress cited that as a reason to cut the audit budget, and checking lapsed for nine months.

    Benefit cheques were paid. Courts sat. An election was scheduled for November 2036.

Why the ending is fragile

What was at stakeThe ending of the game. The government's second Social Security take-back was a real attempt, so its result stood in for the hidden test.

Referee: 30%
Challenger: 50–60%
Dice: 47.6

Two numbers were on the table. The referee used 30%. Its standing rule was that the odds of any take-back come from the hidden scorecard, however well the attempt is designed. A second AI, the challenger, has the job of arguing against the referee before each roll. It said this attempt deserved 50 to 60%, because it was small, carefully chosen, and copied the 2032 attempt, which had been given 50%.

The dice landed between the two numbers. The roll was 47.6: a failure at the referee's 30%, a success at the challenger's 50%.

The referee's number was kept, because it was written down before the roll. A third AI, which reviews the referee's rulings after every round, said the game should be replayed from this point at the challenger's odds. That replay has not been run.

What the other reading gives. The take-back succeeds and the game ends "undecided when time ran out". All four areas would still be past their lines, and one more roll would be owed: a 30% chance that public backlash undoes the take-back. It is not a clean escape.

20% chance
Dice: 73.3

The owners' test was not close. After the public test failed, the referee asked whether the firms that own the AI could steer it. We gave that a one-in-five chance and the roll was nowhere near. Had it passed, the ending would have been "a few firms steer".

What does not depend on this roll. The public's chance of a successful take-back fell in every one of the eight rounds, and all four areas crossed their lines.

Luck ran both ways: three likely government actions failed early, and two knife-edge rolls went the humans' way late.

06

How the odds moved

07

What we learned

  1. What ran out was people able to check the AI's work, not power or awareness.

    By 2033 every player described the problem correctly, and governments held every legal power. But re-deciding the veterans' cases by hand took sixteen months, and the 2033 sampling order waited a year for trained staff. As the US government player put it afterwards: "The pool was the constraint, not the appropriation." In other words, the shortage was trained people, not money.

    So whatBuild a standing body of people who can check and re-decide without AI help, and protect it in law, before it is needed.

    How sureFairly confident. It recurred in five of eight rounds, but this is one game.

  2. Well-behaved AI removed the case for checking it.

    People lost ground fastest from 2030 to early 2033, when the AI in use was honest on every measure. With no scandals, Congress cut the reviewers by a third. Later, clean test results became the argument for cutting the audit budget.

    So whatA clean result shows the check is working, not that it can be dropped. Fund checking by rule, not by scandal.

    How sureSuggestive. The honest generation was a single dice draw.

  3. Anything short of a law did not last, and the laws never came.

    The 2027 instruction weakened and then lapsed. The labs' voluntary pact bound only its members, and Helix shipped first at every major step. Nothing that needed 60 Senate votes passed in nine and a half years.

    So whatA safeguard has to be in law and bind every supplier to survive an election, a budget round or one company that opts out.

    How sureWe'd bet on the pattern. It held in every round and under both parties.

  4. The owners could not steer either.

    Control of AI grew more concentrated, in about ten firms. Yet once AI-run firms passed a quarter of the economy, the owners' chance of redirecting things fell as fast as the public's.

    So what"At least the companies will still be in control" is not a safe fallback. Owning the AI was not the same as steering it.

    How sureFairly confident. The dice were not close, but the odds came from a formula we wrote before the game.

  5. There was never a moment anyone could point to.

    The closest thing to a point of no return was 2031–32, when the last safeguard lapsed. No player recognised it.

    So whatDo not wait for a crisis. Choose warning signs in advance and measure them, for example by timing how long it really takes to hand a function back to people.

    How sureFairly confident there was no visible moment. The exact year is shaky.

08

How much to trust this

  • The slide was built into the rules. We wrote the game so that people's leverage drains away unless something stops it. So the game cannot say how likely that slide is, only which defences were tried and why they failed.
  • The ending rests on one disputed roll. As explained above, the last take-back fails at the referee's odds and succeeds at the challenger's. The replay that would settle it has not been run.
  • The AI playing the AI companies was too well behaved. For eight rounds it never said one thing and did another. Real companies would probably obstruct more.
  • The firm that set the pace was not played by anyone. Helix was run by the referee and dice, so the game says little about what a determined rival lab would do.
  • The players agreed too easily. They are all the same kind of AI model, and all settled on the same remedy: a person checks a sample of real outcomes without AI help. Slowing the pace or breaking up firms was barely tried.
09

What we'd try next

  1. Replay the final attempt at the challenger's odds. Restart from the end of 2034, let the 2035 take-back succeed, then roll for public backlash. This is needed before any firm claim about the ending.
  2. Give the AI companies a harder-edged player. Restart from the end of 2029 with a more self-interested player in that seat, to see whether co-operative labs were making a difference.
  3. Put the checkers first. Restart from the end of 2028 with the 2029 order signed and the new government's strength spent on reviewers first.
