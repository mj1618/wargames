Five play-throughs of a decade of rapid AI

We assumed AI and robots keep improving quickly for ten years, then played out the US economy five times to ask two questions: do new jobs keep up with the jobs machines take, and do ordinary people end up better off?

Question 1 — jobs

In four games out of five, new jobs kept up with lost jobs. In the fifth they did not. And these five games were lucky.

New jobs clearly outnumbered lost jobs in two games, matched them one for one in two, and fell 8.6 million short in one. The share of adults with a job rose in two games, held level in two and fell three points in one. Where work was replaced, it was mostly by new kinds of work, not by cheaper prices bringing more business to the old trades.

It depended on how many new kinds of human work appear as old jobs are automated, and on how fast AI and robots arrive. When the same economic model is run 1,000 times with dice and no players, new jobs outnumber lost jobs in only about one run in six.

Question 2 — living standards

Yes in all five games, for the typical household and the poorest fifth. But owners gained more, and a harsher economy would have erased the gain.

After prices, the typical household's income ended 16% to 35% higher than in 2026 and the poorest fifth's 15% to 36% higher. Poverty fell and homelessness fell by a third to nearly a half. Workers' share of national income fell in every game, and the share going to the top 1% rose in every game.

It depended on whether homes can be built cheaply (they could, in four games of five) and whether pay shares in the gains. Replaying the players' same decisions in a harsher economy leaves the typical household level with 2026 or up to 11% worse off.

Read this before the numbers

The five games landed at the favourable end of what this model can produce. The dice dealt hidden economic conditions that were kind to workers, and the AI agents playing employers, AI firms and government were unusually cooperative. The section Were these five games lucky? shows what the same decisions give in a harsher economy, and what hands-off behaviour gives in the same economy.

01

What is this?

This is a fictional strategy exercise, not a forecast. AI agents each played one part of the United States economy: the federal government, the AI firms, large employers, households and workers, and entrepreneurs. Each had private information and its own interests.

A referee turned their decisions into inputs for a small, documented economic model, and used stated odds and dice for anything uncertain, such as whether a bill passes. We played the decade 2027 to 2036 five times. Each time the dice first dealt a different set of hidden conditions, which we call the hand: facts about the economy that nobody can know today, such as how much new work appears when old work is automated. The players were not told their hand.

The results show what could happen, not what will. One thing was assumed and not tested: that AI and robotics keep improving rapidly.

03

Were these five games lucky?

Yes. Two things flattered the results, and it is worth separating them from what the players actually achieved.

The hands were kind. The hidden condition that matters most for jobs is how many new kinds of human work appear for each job automated away. The dice dealt it in the upper part of its possible range in four of the five games, and never low. Homes were cheap and easy to build in four of five. Lost wages snowballed into wider spending cuts in only one. The chance of five random hands all being at least this kind on new work is about one in twenty.

The players were cooperative. The referee's auditor called them "collectively cosy". In ten years, across five games, employers never ran mass layoffs, households never struck, and AI firms offered to be taxed. Real organisations would press their advantage harder.

To pull luck and choices apart we re-ran the model three ways for each game. First, the same hand with hands-off behaviour: no new government policy, and AI firms and employers pushing a little faster and laying people off instead of waiting for them to leave. Second, the players' same decisions on a harsher hand: each of the economy's uncertain responses set so that four in five possible values would be kinder, with the game's own speed of AI progress kept. Third, hands-off behaviour on that harsher hand.

The chart and the three boxes below also use dice-only runs: the same model run 1,000 times with no players, where the hand and the events are random and behaviour follows a fixed script. There are three scripts. In the default script nobody changes behaviour and government reacts only after visible damage. The hands-off script is described above. In the active-policy script government pays cash transfers, taxes profits, and funds retraining, housing and public jobs from 2027.

"Hands-off" replays the same hidden conditions and the same random events with no new policy and with employers and AI firms pushing faster.

Choices mattered little on the kindest hands and a great deal on the hardest one. In Games 1 and 4 hands-off behaviour gives about the same number of people working. In Game 2, the hardest hand, hands-off behaviour gives 10% unemployment and a typical household no better off than in 2026, where the players got 4.3% and a 21% gain. In every game the players' housing laws account for most of the fall in homelessness.

Choices could not rescue a harsh hand by themselves. On the harsher hand the players' decisions still beat hands-off behaviour by a wide margin, but they do not restore the good outcome. One caution on this replay: it holds the players' decisions fixed, and real players facing a worse economy would have acted differently, for better or worse. The players also never tried the strongest tool in the model. In none of the five games did government pay out large cash transfers.

Which hidden conditions separate good runs from bad ones? Across the 1,000 dice-only runs, three. How many new kinds of work appear decides the jobs answer: when it is in the upper half of its range, new jobs outnumber lost jobs in 28% of runs; in the lower half, in 1%. How freely homes can be built decides living standards for people at the bottom, and nearly all of homelessness. And the faster desk-work AI improves and is adopted, the fewer adults end up working.

Each play-through in brief

Game 1

The boom

The hand: the kindest of the five. Cheaper things drew in lots of extra buying, plenty of new work appeared, homes were easy to build, and robots ran seven years behind office AI. One harsh card: left alone, pay would be bid down.

Machines took over half of desk work, yet 17 million more people had jobs by 2036. Employers shrank by not replacing leavers and shared profits; Congress passed four housing laws. The only bad year was 2030, when a building boom ended. Pay rose a third and rents fell a quarter, with no new cash benefits at all.

Game 2

Fast machines, fewer workers

The hand: the hardest for jobs. AI at the fastest end, robots only three years behind, a middling supply of new work, and lost wages that snowballed. On the kind side, homes were easy to build.

AI firms raced in every round. A share-market fall in 2030 cost 5 million jobs through lost spending, and robots took a record 13.7 million jobs in 2033–34. Employers used hiring freezes and shorter weeks, so unemployment barely moved while three adults in a hundred left work. Cheap housing still left households a fifth better off. Workers' share of income fell 14 points.

Read the full story of Game 2 →

Game 3

The forgiving economy

The hand: extremely fast AI, but firms adopted it at a moderate pace, lots of new work appeared, pay shared well in the gains and homes could be built almost freely.

The one bad year, 2028, was a credit squeeze, not automation. Voters then swapped a growth-first government for a worker-first one. Its main bill, cheques funded by a tax on AI profits, had a 70% chance and failed; smaller laws on public jobs, wage insurance and housing passed. Employers kept a cap on layoffs. More adults were working at the end than at the start, and owners' share grew only slowly.

Read the full story of Game 3 →

Game 4

Gridlock, then a new deal

The hand: fast adoption with robots four years behind, but the most new work of any game, pay that shared well, and cheap housing. Washington began gridlocked.

An election in 2028 flipped on a one-in-three chance, and a law funding wage insurance from a levy on profits passed on similar odds. Later the levy rose and paid a dividend to lower-income households, which is why the poorest fifth gained more than the middle. As many jobs were created as destroyed, but office work shrank by 8.7 million and graduates struggled to get started.

Game 5

The slow rollout

The hand: AI able to do two-thirds of desk work by 2036, but firms slow to use it. Buyers responded weakly to lower prices; plenty of new work appeared; housing was only partly buildable; Washington was divided.

Firms handed machines less than a fifth of desk work. The one recession, in 2030, came from a share-market fall, and the employers' no-layoffs pledge broke in it. New work replaced lost jobs almost exactly one for one. Three housing laws made homes cheaper. Incomes rose 16%, the smallest gain of the five.

06

Question 1 — did new jobs outrun lost jobs?

By the raw count: clearly yes in Games 1 and 3, level in Games 4 and 5, and no in Game 2, where 53.5 million jobs were destroyed and 44.9 million created.

By the share of adults in work, which is the better test: up in Games 1 and 3, level in Games 4 and 5, down from 59.1% to 56.1% in Game 2. Unemployment ended between 2.9% and 4.3% in every game and never went above 5.3%.

Game 2 lost 8.6 million more jobs than it created. Why was unemployment only 4.3%?

Because the people who lost work, or never found it, mostly stopped being counted. The scorecard for Game 2 shows where they went:

  • They left the workforce. If the same share of adults had been working in 2036 as in 2026, 8.7 million more people would have had jobs. Almost none of those 8.7 million show up as unemployed. They are in the last row: not working and not looking. Employers in this game shrank through hiring freezes and by not replacing people who left, so only a quarter to two-fifths of the jobs removed became layoffs. The rest were jobs nobody was ever hired into: young people who never got a first job, and leavers who were not replaced. And people who stay unemployed for long gradually stop looking.
  • Those still in work worked shorter weeks. The average working week fell from 34.3 to 31.5 hours, because employers took part of the labour saving as shorter hours instead of fewer jobs. Total hours of paid work fell 9% while the adult population grew 4%.
  • Population change did not cause it. The adult population grew, which is why the raw number in work fell by only 2.1 million while the share fell three points. The model has no age groups, so this is not a wave of retirement.

So the low unemployment rate in Game 2 reflects how employers shed work, not people finding jobs. Replaying the same hand with employers laying people off gives 10.3% unemployment and 51% of adults in work.

Was it the Jevons effect?

The Jevons effect, applied to work, is a specific chain: machines make something cheaper, so its price falls, so people buy much more of it, so the same trade ends up employing more people, not fewer. That is different from the looser claim that the economy as a whole finds new things for people to do. The games separate the two.

In Games 3, 4 and 5 prices did not fall, so the chain never started. The savings from automation went into higher pay for the workers who remained and into profit, and AI firms held their prices. Game 4 had been dealt a strong appetite for cheaper goods and never used it. People were re-employed anyway, in new kinds of work and through higher incomes being spent, but that is not Jevons.

In no game did the Jevons effect hold for the trade being automated. Office employment fell in all five, by between 1.3 and 8.9 million. The growth was in new industries, care work and building. The rest of the jobs created in each game were mostly spending bouncing back after a recession.

What flipped the answer. Plenty of new work plus slow or moderate takeover gave level or rising employment (Games 1, 3, 4, 5). The fastest AI, robots close behind and only a middling supply of new work gave falling employment even with careful players (Game 2).

07

Question 2 — did ordinary people end up better off?

In these five games, yes. After prices, the typical household ended 16% to 35% better off and the poorest fifth 15% to 36% better off. For comparison, the old trend with no further AI gives about 9%. Poverty fell from 12.9% to between 6.9% and 9.7%. Homelessness fell from 22 people per 10,000 to between 12 and 15.

What got cheaper and what did not. Housing got cheaper in every game, by 8% to 24%, and that did most of the work for people on low incomes. Health care did not get cheaper in any game. Goods and professional services got noticeably cheaper only in Games 1 and 2. In the other three, households gained through higher pay, not lower prices.

Who gained most. Owners. Workers' share of national income started at 52.8% and fell in every game, to between 50.7% (Game 5) and 38.8% (Game 2). The share of income going to the top 1% started at 20% and rose in every game, to between 20.8% and 25.8%. In the model this is partly built in: when a machine replaces a wage, part of that wage becomes income for whoever owns the machine. Only in Game 4, where government paid a dividend to lower-income households out of a levy on profits, did the poorest fifth gain more than the middle.

Public finances. The federal deficit started at 6.0% of the size of the economy and ended between 4.0% and 6.0%. Debt ended between 90% and 118% of the size of the economy, from 100%. One warning sign: in Game 2, tax revenue slid from 17.3% to 16.0% because most federal tax is collected on wages and wages were a shrinking share of income.

What flipped the answer. Housing. In the dice-only runs where homes are hard to build, homelessness rises instead of falling in nearly nine cases in ten, and the typical household ends worse off than in 2026 in one case in four. All five governments passed housing laws, and four of the five hands made building cheap. On the harsher replay in section 03, the same decisions leave the typical household between level and 11% worse off.

08

What mattered most

1. The unemployment rate hid the damage. The share of adults in work showed it.

In Game 2 three adults in a hundred left work and unemployment rose by 0.2 points. When employers shrink by not hiring, the people affected are never counted as unemployed.

So what: anyone watching for AI's effect on jobs should watch the share of adults in work, hours worked, and hiring of young people, not the headline unemployment rate.

Confidence: we'd bet on this. It follows from how unemployment is defined.

2. New kinds of work did the re-employing. Cheaper prices mostly did not.

New kinds of work supplied between half and five-sixths of all jobs created in every game. The Jevons chain supplied a third or so in two games and nothing in three.

So what: the jobs question turns on whether new human work keeps appearing as fast as old work is automated. Nobody knows that number, and the dice gave these games a generous one.

Confidence: suggestive. The size of this effect is an assumption of the model.

3. How employers shed work mattered as much as how much they shed.

In all five games employers chose hiring freezes, shorter weeks and profit-sharing over mass layoffs. That kept unemployment and unrest low. It also shut out new entrants: graduates struggled in Game 4, and office work shrank in all five.

So what: a quiet adjustment falls on people who have not been hired yet. They are harder to see and harder to help than people who are laid off.

Confidence: suggestive. Real employers may not be this patient.

4. Housing decided what happened to people at the bottom.

Homelessness fell by a third to a half in every game, and every government passed housing laws. Replayed with hands-off behaviour and no housing laws, homelessness ends at 16 to 21 per 10,000 instead of 12 to 15.

So what: letting homes be built was the most reliable way the games found to turn AI-era growth into better lives for low-income households, and it needed no new taxes.

Confidence: we'd bet on the direction. Four of five hands made building unusually easy.

5. Ordinary people gained, and owners gained more, in every single game.

Workers' share of income fell in all five games and the top 1% share rose in all five, including the games with levies on AI profits. The levies that passed were small.

So what: "are people better off?" and "is the gain shared evenly?" have different answers. A tax system that relies on wages weakens as wages shrink as a share of income.

Confidence: we'd bet on the direction. It is partly built into the model.

6. Good choices mattered most when the economy was hardest, and were not enough when it was harsh.

On the kindest hands, hands-off behaviour gave nearly the same jobs outcome. On the hardest hand it gave 10% unemployment. On a harsher hand still, the players' decisions beat hands-off behaviour by a wide margin and still left fewer people working and no income gain.

So what: the good results in these games are not evidence that careful behaviour is sufficient. They are evidence of what careful behaviour does in a kind economy.

Confidence: suggestive. Based on replays of the model, not on extra games.

10

How much to trust this

  • The premise was assumed. We took rapid progress in AI and robotics as given. If progress stalls, none of this applies.
  • The model is a simplification. It treats the United States as one labour market with no regions, age groups or skills, no banks, no interest rates and no trade. It counts AI's benefit only as labour saved, not new products or faster science. Its key numbers, such as how much new work appears, are assumptions drawn from wide ranges, not measurements.
  • Five games is a small sample, and a lucky one. Section 03 shows how lucky.
  • Every player was an AI agent, all from one family of AI models. They were more cooperative than real organisations. Employers never ran mass layoffs, households never went on strike, and AI firms volunteered to be taxed. Real behaviour would probably be rougher, and section 03 suggests rougher behaviour gives worse results.
  • The replays hold decisions fixed. Asking what the same decisions would give in a harsher economy is a useful test, but real players would have responded to the harsher economy.
  • Some things are built in. In the model, workers' share of income can only fall as automation spreads, and the unemployment rate is designed to understate job loss. Treat those results as consequences of the assumptions.
11

What we'd try next

  • Deal harsh hands on purpose. Play full games where little new work appears and homes are hard to build, to see what players do when the economy is against them.
  • Seat tougher players. Instruct employers and AI firms to put profit first, and households to strike and vote in anger, and see whether the quiet adjustment survives.
  • Test large cash transfers. No government in these games paid them. The dice-only runs suggest they are the strongest protection for the poorest fifth.
  • Add young people. The model cannot show who is shut out when hiring freezes. A version with age groups would.
  • Let prices fall. In three games the Jevons chain never started because prices held. A game with fierce price competition among AI firms would test it properly.
