The outcome in one sentence

Machines took over almost half of desk work in ten years, yet as many jobs were created as destroyed; household incomes rose by about a third and poverty nearly halved, but office jobs shrank and workers' share of national income fell to a record low.

1

What is this?

This is one of five play-throughs of the same fictional exercise about what rapid progress in AI and robots does to jobs and living standards in the United States between 2027 and 2036. AI agents played the federal government, the AI companies, large employers, households and entrepreneurs, each with private information. A referee, dice and a small documented economic model kept score, so this shows what could happen under one set of conditions, not what will.

2

The hand this run was dealt

Before each play-through, dice set hidden facts about how the economy works. The players were not told them. This run drew a fast-moving but forgiving economy.

In this economy's favour

  • Many new kinds of human work appear as AI spreads.
  • When workers produce more, their pay rises with it.
  • Homes can be built quickly and cheaply.
  • When some people lose wages, business investment keeps spending going, so the loss does not snowball.
  • People buy a lot more of anything that gets cheaper. This went almost unused, because prices barely fell.

Against it

  • AI for desk work improves very fast and firms adopt it quickly, so disruption arrives early.
  • Robots for physical work are only about four years behind.
  • Washington starts deadlocked, led by a coalition that favours fast AI rollout and dislikes new taxes.

One random event was drawn each round. In order: a court ruling that made firms legally responsible for decisions taken by their automated systems (2027); capable AI models given away free (2028); a leap in what AI can do (2029); a safety recall of robots (2030); a scare in the government bond market (2031–32); a state wage-insurance scheme shown to work (2033–34); and a consumer boom in cheap AI-assisted services (2035–36).

3

What happened

  1. 1
    2027

    A court ruling slows the machines

    The liability ruling cut the pace of automation by about a fifth. Congress tried to give firms legal protection and failed. Employers shrank by freezing hiring, not by laying people off. About 1.5 million jobs went and 0.5 million appeared. Unemployment edged up to 4.3%, and graduates could not get a first job.

  2. 2
    2028

    Free AI, and a change of government

    With free AI models everywhere, employers began automating across whole companies, still quietly: 2.0 million jobs went and 1.5 million were created. Unemployment reached 4.4%, its highest point of the decade. In November voters removed the governing coalition. We gave that about a one-in-three chance and the dice came up inside it. A worker-first coalition now led a Congress that was still deadlocked.

  3. 3
    2029

    A leap in AI, and one law gets through

    A new generation of AI could run week-long projects alone. Machines could now do 30% of desk work and firms used them for 11%. Jobs destroyed (2.6 million) still outran jobs created (2.4 million). Congress passed the Worker Transition Act: wage insurance (a top-up for people who lose a job and take a lower-paid one), apprenticeships and shorter-hours schemes, paid for by an extra 3-percentage-point tax on company profits. It had about a 30% chance. Had it failed, the later support for poorer households would have had no funding source.

  4. 4
    2030

    The robot recall

    A safety recall froze automation of physical work for a year. For the first time more jobs were created (2.6 million) than destroyed (2.1 million), and unemployment fell back to 4.1%. The profits tax began.

  5. 5
    2031–32

    A cheque for lower-income households

    Jobs destroyed and created were level at 5.2 million each. A less deadlocked Congress raised the profits tax to 7 points, used it to pay a yearly dividend to the lower-income two-fifths of households, and funded house-building. From here the poorest fifth's income grew faster than the typical household's. Poverty fell below 10%.

  6. 6
    2033–34

    Robots arrive at scale

    The share of physical work done by machines doubled from 6% to 12%, and losses spread from offices to shops, factories and warehouses. In two years 7.4 million jobs went and 6.9 million were created. The share of adults in work touched its low of 58.4%.

  7. 7
    2035–36

    A boom in cheap services with people in them

    Cheap services that pair AI with a human, such as a $40-a-month tutor-and-lawyer subscription, caught on. In two years 10.3 million jobs were created against 8.6 million destroyed, the best balance of the decade. Unemployment ended at 3.9%. Congress raised the profits tax to 10 points and nearly doubled the dividend.

The decade in five charts

5

Did new jobs outrun lost jobs here?

They kept pace, no more. About 29.5 million jobs were destroyed and 29.6 million created. Total employment still rose by 6 million, because the population grew.

The classic argument, often called the Jevons effect, is that when machines make something cheaper, people buy so much more of it that the trade ends up employing more people. That barely happened here. Prices of goods fell by under 2% and services not at all, because AI companies held their prices and higher pay absorbed most of the savings. Of the 29.6 million new jobs, about 25 million were new kinds of work, 3 million came from households having more to spend, and 1 million from displaced people taking lower-paid jobs.

Who lost out. Office work fell from 48.0 million jobs to 39.3 million, a loss of 8.7 million. Graduates were shut out for years, because employers cut by not hiring. The growth was in new businesses built around AI (up 8.1 million), care work (up 4.3 million) and construction (up 2.4 million), so a displaced office worker usually had to change trade.

6

Did ordinary people end up better off here?

Yes, broadly. The typical household's income after prices rose 30%; without AI the long-run trend would have given about 9%. The poorest fifth gained 36%. Poverty fell from 12.9% to 6.9% of people. Homelessness fell from 22 to 12 in every 10,000. Housing costs fell 19%, while health care got slightly dearer and other prices barely moved.

But owners gained more than workers. The economy grew 42%. The share of national income paid as wages fell from 52.8% to 46.5%, a record low, and the top 1% raised their share of all income from 20.0% to 22.2%.

7

What drove it

The dealt hand did most of the work. Plentiful new work, pay that kept up and cheap housing would have produced a good average under most choices. When we replayed this same hand and the same events with a hands-off government and employers, the typical household still gained about 32% and 58.6% of adults were in work.

The players' choices decided who shared in it. In that hands-off replay the poorest fifth gained 24%, not 36%; homelessness ended at 16 per 10,000, not 12; and unemployment ended at 4.9%, not 3.9%. Three choices mattered:

  • Employers cut through hiring freezes and shorter weeks, not mass layoffs. Unemployment stayed low, and graduates paid for it.
  • The government's profits tax, dividend and housing money lifted the poorest fifth above the typical household.
  • AI companies held their prices, so the savings showed up as profit and pay, not cheaper shopping.

The dice decided the politics. The 2028 election turned on about a one-in-three chance and the Worker Transition Act passed on similar odds. Six of seven union organising drives succeeded at even odds each. The final-round consumer boom added about 3 million jobs: replayed without any random events, this run creates 26.4 million jobs against 28.7 million destroyed.

8

Where the players were unrealistic

  • Households never went on strike, even with wages' share of income at a record low and unions winning almost every organising drive.
  • AI companies stopped fighting a profits tax that more than tripled, from 3 points to 10.
  • Industry and government missed the same bargain six times (legal protection for firms in exchange for a company-funded worker fund), and kept trying.
  • By 2033 all five players backed the same wage-insurance model.

What that means for trusting the result. The players were all AI agents from one model family, and they were more cooperative and public-spirited than real firms, unions and politicians. With more conflict, expect more layoffs, higher unemployment and less money reaching the poorest. The hand was also lucky: for typical household income this run beat more than 99% of 1,000 runs of the same model played by dice alone. The model is simple, with no regions, ages or financial system. Read this page as "what a kind economy and unusually reasonable people can produce", not as a forecast.
