The outcome in one sentence

By 2036 AI could do two-thirds of desk work, but employers had handed over less than a fifth of it, new kinds of work replaced the lost jobs almost exactly one for one, and most households ended up better off, while office work shrank and workers' share of national income slipped.

1

What is this?

This is one of five play-throughs of the same fictional exercise, which asks what rapidly improving AI and robots do to American jobs and living standards between 2027 and 2036. AI agents played the federal government, the AI companies, large employers, households and entrepreneurs. A referee, dice and a documented economic model kept score, so this shows what could happen under one set of assumptions, not what will.

The hand this run was dealt

Hidden from the players

Before each play-through, dice set a dozen facts about how the economy works. The players never saw them. This run got a kind economy hidden behind an alarming rise in what AI could do.

What was favourable

  • Employers adopted AI slowly, far behind what it could do.
  • Many new kinds of human work appeared, almost one for each job automated.
  • Pay captured a large part of the productivity gains.
  • Lost wages did not snowball into wider slumps.

What was not

  • AI for desk work improved very fast.
  • When things got cheaper, people did not buy much more of them.
  • Washington was divided and slow. Big bills usually failed.
  • The central bank would not cut interest rates to cushion a downturn.

In between

  • Robots ran about six years behind desk-work AI.
  • Savings from automation were split between lower prices and higher profits.
  • New housing only partly kept up with demand.

Dice also drew five random events:

  • 2027A sudden leap in what AI could do.
  • 2028A boom in cheap AI-delivered services such as tutoring and legal help.
  • 2030A fall in the share market and a squeeze on lending.
  • 2031–32A fall in the cost of building homes.
  • 2035–36A shortage of land in the cities where the AI industry clustered.
3

What happened

7 rounds
  1. 1
    2027

    A loud year in the headlines, a quiet one in the numbers

    The share of desk work that AI could do jumped from 6% to 16%. Employers handed over only about 2%, by freezing hiring, not by firing. Unemployment stayed at 4.1%.

    The dice went against Washington: a retraining bill with a 55% chance failed, and a housing bill passed at half size.

  2. 2
    2028

    More jobs made than lost

    Households bought cheap AI tutoring and legal help, and new firms hired people to supply them. About 0.9 million jobs were created against 0.6 million lost. Unemployment fell to 4.0%.

  3. 3
    2029

    Washington finally legislates

    The AI companies put $2 billion for workers into a trust, to be released once Congress passed the law they wanted. It passed: states could not write their own AI licensing rules, and firms had to report AI layoffs. A funded housing law passed too. But the 2028 spending burst faded: 1.1 million jobs were lost and 0.7 million created.

  4. 4
    2030

    The one bad year

    AI company shares fell by a third and banks cut lending. Households saved more, and the central bank did not help. About 4.9 million jobs were lost: 4.2 million because spending fell, only 0.7 million to machines. Unemployment hit 5.3%, and the poorest fifth's income dipped below its 2026 level.

    Employers had pledged to avoid layoffs. We gave the pledge a 60% chance of holding. It broke.

  5. 5
    2031–32

    The jobs come back, and the government loses anyway

    Lending returned and 5.7 million jobs were created, 3.5 million of them from spending recovering. Unemployment fell back to about 4% and pay rose about 8%.

    A bill to send households cheques, paid for by a levy on AI profits, had about a 30% chance and failed. Then the pro-growth coalition that had led since 2027 lost the 2032 election on a one-in-four roll, to a coalition that put workers first.

  6. 6
    2033–34

    Quiet and prosperous

    Machines took 2.4 million jobs and 2.8 million were created, most in new kinds of work. A levy on profits, with cash for the poorer two-fifths of households, passed at half size. Poverty fell to 10.4%.

  7. 7
    2035–36

    The fastest automation yet, still matched

    Machines took 3.0 million jobs in two years and 3.2 million were created. The land shortage nudged housing costs up, and a new housing law (a 60% chance, which came up) limited the damage. Unemployment ended at 4.0%.

The numbers over time

From the run's scorecard
5

Did new jobs outrun lost jobs here?

They kept pace, no more. About 14.2 million jobs were lost over ten years and about 14.2 million were created.

Of the jobs lost, 9.7 million went to machines and 4.5 million to falling spending, nearly all in 2030. Of the jobs created, 8.9 million were new kinds of work, 4.3 million came from spending recovering, and 1.0 million from slow re-hiring. Employment rose from 162.4 million to 168.9 million only because the population grew.

The hopeful theory is that when machines make something cheaper, people buy so much more of it that the trade ends up employing more people, not fewer. That did not happen here. Prices barely fell and buyers responded weakly, so almost no jobs came from cheaper prices. The work was replaced by different work.

Who lost out: office workers as a group. Office jobs fell from 48.0 million to 46.0 million while care, building, retail and new industries grew. Openings for office juniors thinned out, and the people laid off in 2030 lost a year or two.

6

Did ordinary people end up better off here?

Yes, broadly. The typical household's income rose 16% after prices and the poorest fifth's rose 15%.

Hourly pay rose 19% after prices. Housing became 8% cheaper. Poverty fell from 12.9% to 9.7% of people, and homelessness from 22 to 14.6 per 10,000.

What did not improve: the things automation was expected to make cheap. Goods and digital services cost less than 1% less, and health care cost slightly more. The top 1% raised their share of income from 20.0% to 20.8%, and wages fell from 52.8% to 50.7% of national income. The government's yearly deficit ended where it began, at 6.0% of the economy, while its debt grew from 100% to 118%.

7

What drove it

Mostly the hand that was dealt. The players' choices mattered at the edges, and the dice set the timing.

Mattered most

The dealt hand

Slow adoption meant AI did 18% of desk work in 2036 when it could have done 67%. Plentiful new work and well-shared pay did the rest.

Three decisions

The players' choices

Entrepreneurs kept backing firms that hire people. Washington passed several housing laws, which is why housing got cheaper. Employers chose hiring freezes and shorter weeks over layoffs, except in 2030.

Timing

The dice

Dice produced the 2030 slump, broke the no-layoffs pledge, sank the cheques bill and decided the 2032 election.

8

Where the players were unrealistic

An independent checker reviewed the referee's work during the game and called the players "very cooperative".

  • Households never struck or boycotted in ten years. Their union organising drives failed five rounds running, and they still never escalated.
  • Employers paid for shorter working weeks without being pushed.
  • The AI companies volunteered a levy on their own profits.
  • The referee made small scoring slips. The checker found two settings slightly too pessimistic and one leaning optimistic. None changed the picture.

So read this as the friendly end of what this hand could produce. The headline result rests mainly on the hidden conditions, which good behaviour did not create. But the calm politics and the lack of conflict owe something to players who were nicer to each other than real organisations tend to be.
