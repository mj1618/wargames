The United States, 2027 to 2036, with AI improving quickly the whole time.

The outcome in one sentence

Machines came to do about half of all desk work, yet 17 million more people had jobs, pay went a third further and rents fell by nearly a quarter. Workers still ended up with a smaller slice of a much bigger pie.

What is this?

This is one of five play-throughs of the same fictional exercise, which asks what ten years of rapidly improving AI and robots would do to jobs and living standards in the United States. AI agents played the federal government, the AI companies, large employers, households and entrepreneurs, each with its own goals and private information. A referee, dice and a small, documented economic model kept score, so this shows one way things could go, not a forecast.

The hand this run was dealt

Hidden from the players

Before each play-through, dice set a dozen facts about how the economy behaves, which nobody yet knows about the real world. The players were not told them. This run drew a kind hand with one harsh card in it.

What was favourable

  • When things got cheaper, people bought a lot more of them.
  • Many new kinds of human work appeared.
  • Competition passed most cost savings on to customers as lower prices.
  • Robots ran about seven years behind desk-work AI, so physical jobs were mostly safe.
  • Homes were physically quick and cheap to build.
  • Lost wages did not snowball into wider slumps.
  • Washington could pass laws within a couple of years.

What was not

  • Desk-work AI improved very fast and firms adopted it quickly, so office jobs went early.
  • The harsh card: left alone, pay would be bid down below its old trend instead of sharing in the gains.
  • The central bank was strict: no help in a downturn, and a squeeze whenever unemployment got very low.
  • The big states refused to help build housing all decade.
  • The coalition leading Washington favoured fast AI rollout and low taxes, and was wary of new benefits.

Random events that came up

  • 2027: a flat year for AI progress.
  • 2028: a breakthrough in factory-built housing.
  • 2029: a data-centre building boom.
  • 2030: many of the new jobs turned out to be automatable too.
  • 2031–32: a price war started by free AI models anyone could run.
  • 2033–34: a shortage of land in boom cities.
  • 2035–36: a hit new industry.
3

What happened

Seven rounds, ten years
  1. 1
    2027

    A quiet year on the surface

    Employers automated routine office work, but shrank by not replacing people who left instead of by layoffs. About 1.2 million jobs went and 1.1 million appeared, so unemployment stayed at 4.1%. Congress passed incentives for house-building. A court struck down a rule making firms report AI layoffs, which the referee had given a 35% chance.

  2. 2
    2028

    Rents start to fall

    The housing breakthrough, on top of the new incentives, cut housing costs by 5% in a year. New firms hired heavily: 2.5 million jobs were created against 1.8 million lost. Unemployment fell to 3.8%, and voters re-elected the governing coalition.

  3. 3
    2029

    The boom runs hot

    The data-centre building boom added about 1.3 million jobs. In all, 4.2 million jobs were created against 2.4 million lost, and unemployment fell to 3.2%. A union organising drive succeeded for the second year running, a coin-flip each time. The AI companies tied large customers into five-year exclusive contracts.

  4. 4
    2030

    The only bad year

    The building boom ended and the central bank tightened. 5.7 million jobs went, only 2.4 million of them to automation, and 2.9 million were created. The number of people in work fell by 2.2 million. Unemployment only reached 3.7%, because most of those who lost work stopped looking and so were not counted. Pay stood still.

  5. 5
    2031–32

    Free AI, cheaper services, a wave of new work

    Free AI models forced a price war. The AI companies ended their exclusive contracts and gave their basic service away. 10.6 million jobs were created against 6.9 million lost, and unemployment fell to 3.0%. Every move on the workers' side failed on the dice, including a small levy on AI profits that had a one-in-four chance. It made little difference: the typical household's income after prices jumped from 108 to 118 (2026 = 100).

  6. 6
    2033–34

    A land squeeze, and an upset election

    Land ran short in the boom cities. Congress overrode local planning rules there; the referee gave the bill a 90% chance and the roll came up just inside it. Housing costs still fell 2%. 11.7 million jobs were created against 9.1 million lost. Then voters handed Congress to a rival coalition that put worker protections first, which had only a 15% chance in a boom.

  7. 7
    2035–36

    More work than workers

    The hit new industry offered so much work that 3.7 million openings went unfilled. 14.9 million jobs were created against 10.6 million lost. The new Congress passed a levy of two percentage points on AI profits, a 75% chance, and a court let it stand. By the end of 2036 machines did 49% of desk work and robots 10% of physical work.

The decade in charts

From the run's scorecard
5

Did new jobs outrun lost jobs here?

Yes, for the economy as a whole. No, for office workers.

Over ten years 37.6 million jobs were destroyed and 47.9 million created. About 30 million of the openings were new kinds of work, and about 18 million came from cheaper goods and services selling more. The share of adults with a job rose from 59.1% to 62.7%, and the average working week shortened by an hour and a half.

Offices were the exception. They lost 19.3 million jobs and regained 16.1 million, and ended 1.3 million smaller than in 2026. The growth was elsewhere: new industries tripled from 4.6 to 13.9 million jobs, care work added 4.6 million and building 2.8 million. Someone whose office job disappeared usually had to change trade to find the next one.

6

Did ordinary people end up better off here?

Yes, broadly, including the poorest. But the owners of capital gained more.

After prices, the typical household's income rose 35%; without the AI boom the model's trend gives about 9%. The poorest fifth gained 32.5%. Housing became 23% cheaper and professional services 13% cheaper. Goods fell only 5% and healthcare barely moved. Poverty fell from 12.9% to 7.3%. The government paid out no new cash benefits, and its deficit shrank from 6% to 4% of the economy.

Who lost out: the economy grew 56%, so households received well under their share of it. Wages fell from 52.8% of national income to 45.8%, and the top 1% went from 20% of all income to 22.9%. Many office workers lost their trade, and in 2030 about two million people left work, most without ever being counted as unemployed.

7

What drove it

Mostly the hand. The same model was run 1,000 times with dice alone. This result sits at or above the top of that range for jobs and typical income. When this exact hand was replayed with hands-off players, unemployment still ended at 2.9% and the same share of adults had jobs. The jobs result was dealt, not earned.

The players' choices improved the split. In that hands-off replay the typical household gained 29% instead of 35%, homelessness ended at 16 per 10,000 instead of 12, and wages' share at 43.7% instead of 45.8%. Three choices did it. Employers shrank by not replacing leavers and shared profits with staff, which, with union gains, cancelled the dealt squeeze on pay. Washington passed four housing bills that got round the reluctant states. The AI companies dropped their exclusive contracts in 2031.

Dice changed the timing more than the direction. They made 2030 bad and 2035–36 exceptional. Replaying the players' decisions with no random events gives almost the same ending.

8

Where the players were unrealistic

All five players sat inside one polite bargain for a decade. Employers volunteered profit-sharing and never ran mass layoffs. The AI companies offered to be taxed. Households never panicked or struck in earnest. Real firms and workers would have pressed their advantage harder. An independent checker also found the referee twice read the AI companies' pricing a little generously.

What that means for trust: the jobs numbers lean on the dealt hand and would survive rougher players. The gains for the poorest, the fall in homelessness and the hold on wages lean on good behaviour that real firms might not offer. Treat this as close to a best case, not a typical one.
