What is this?

This is one of five play-throughs of the same fictional exercise, which asks what ten years of rapidly improving AI and robots would do to jobs and living standards in the United States. AI agents played the federal government, the AI companies, large employers, households and entrepreneurs, each with private information. A referee, dice and a documented economic model kept score.

2

The hand this run was dealt

Each play-through starts by rolling hidden conditions that the players never see. This one was dealt a hard economy for jobs, with a soft floor under the cost of living.

What was unfavourable

  • AI for desk work improved at the fastest end of the range, with capable robots only about three years behind.
  • Companies were quick to put the machines to use.
  • Pay did not rise in step with what workers produced.
  • Lost wages snowballed: people who lost pay spent less, which cost others their jobs.

What was middling

  • Cheaper things sold moderately more (a 10% price cut lifted sales about 12%).
  • Some new kinds of human work appeared, not a flood.
  • Savings from automation were split between lower prices and profit.

What was favourable

  • Homes could be built freely and cheaply, and big states were already overriding local planning limits.
  • The central bank was willing to cut interest rates in a slump.
  • Washington could act within a couple of years, led by a "growth-first" coalition: keen on AI and building, wary of new taxes and benefits.

One random event was drawn each round: new jobs that turned out to be automatable themselves (2027); a leap in AI ability (2028); a boom in cheap AI services (2029); a financial wobble (2030); a correction to official jobs figures (2031–32); a shortage of building land in boom cities (2033–34); and a state wage-insurance scheme that worked well (2035–36).

3

What happened

  1. 1
    2027

    The AI companies race, and employers stop hiring

    The AI companies raced to release better systems and large employers rolled them out. About 2.3 million jobs went, half of them in offices; 1.0 million appeared. Employers froze hiring instead of firing, so unemployment only rose from 4.1% to 4.3%. Congress passed grants for house building.

  2. 2
    2028

    A leap in AI, and a half-size safety net

    Another 3.9 million jobs went and 2.4 million appeared; over the two years employment fell 1.5 million. Congress passed, at half size, a 2.5% tax on AI companies' profits to fund wage insurance (a top-up for people who take a lower-paid job). The growth-first coalition kept its lead in the election.

  3. 3
    2029

    The one easy year

    A boom in cheap AI services arrived just as entrepreneurs pushed into businesses that hire lots of people. 6.2 million jobs were created against 3.7 million lost. Unemployment fell to 3.5% and a larger housing law passed.

  4. 4
    2030

    The slump

    AI shares fell by a third just as the previous year's spending boost ran out. 9.0 million jobs disappeared, 5.3 million of them because people were spending less, not because a machine took the work. Unemployment rose only to 4.5%, its peak, because most people who lost work stopped looking: adults in work fell from 59.0% to 56.9%. Household incomes slipped back; rents kept falling.

  5. 5
    2031–32

    Recovery, without a cash payment

    The central bank cut rates and spending came back. 13.5 million jobs were created against 9.9 million lost. A cash payment to the poorer two-fifths of households, funded by a tax rise, failed in Congress; we had given it a one-in-five chance. The AI profits tax expired.

  6. 6
    2033–34

    Robots arrive in force

    13.7 million jobs were destroyed in two years, the most of any period, and 10.1 million appeared. A bill to bring back the AI profits tax had about a one-in-three chance and failed. Housing costs fell despite the land shortage.

  7. 7
    2035–36

    Still losing ground on jobs, still gaining on income

    Another 11.0 million jobs went and 8.6 million appeared. Hourly pay ended 30% above 2026 after prices, but the working week had shrunk from 34.3 to 31.5 hours. A 2% tax on AI profits passed at half its proposed size, with federal money to match state wage insurance.

Charts

5

Did new jobs outrun lost jobs here?

No. Over ten years 53.5 million jobs were destroyed and 44.9 million created. About half the new jobs were new kinds of work; the rest came from cheaper products selling more and from spending recovering after slumps. At the end 160.3 million people had jobs, 2.1 million fewer than in 2026 even though the population had grown.

The damage showed up not as unemployment but as people leaving the workforce: the share of adults working or looking for work fell from 61.6% to 58.6%.

New work did outrun lost work in some places. Care jobs grew from 38.0 to 41.0 million, building from 14.0 to 15.8 million, and newly created industries from 4.6 to 11.7 million. Office and professional jobs fell from 48.0 to 39.1 million, and factories, transport and shops all shrank.

6

Did ordinary people end up better off here?

Mostly yes, on money and housing; no, on their share of the gains. The typical household's income after prices ended 21% above 2026. Without any AI, the old trend would have given about 9%. The poorest fifth gained 19%. Housing costs fell 24%, professional services 12% and goods 7%. Poverty fell from 12.9% to 9.2%, and homelessness from 22 to 13 per 10,000 people.

But the economy grew 52%, far more than household incomes did. The top 1% raised their share of all income from 20% to 25.8%, and wages' share of national income fell from 52.8% to 38.8%.

Who lost out: office and professional workers, who lost about 9 million jobs, and the roughly three in a hundred adults who left work altogether. The government's tax take also slid from 17.3% to 16.0% of the economy, as there were fewer wages to tax.

7

What drove it

Mostly the dealt hand

  • Very fast AI, early robots and quick adoption set the scale of the job losses.
  • Easy house building produced most of the gain in living standards.

Three choices by the players

  • The AI companies raced in every round, so by 2034 machines were able to do 90% of desk work.
  • Employers cut jobs by not replacing leavers, shortened the week and shared profits, which kept the losses out of the unemployment rate.
  • Washington passed four housing laws, but never a meaningful cash payment to households or a lasting tax on AI profits.

The dice

  • The 2030 financial wobble was a random event. It caused the one year when incomes fell.
  • The growth-first coalition faced four elections, each with a one-in-four to one-in-three chance of losing its lead. Surviving all four had only about a one-in-five chance. A different coalition might have taxed and shared more.
  • The final AI profits tax had about a one-in-seven chance of passing whole and the same again of passing at half size. The roll landed just inside the half-size range.
8

Where the players were unrealistic

  • The companies were too polite. Wages' share of income fell 14 points, yet employers never ran mass layoffs and the AI companies offered to be taxed.
  • Households were too calm. They never went on strike, boycotted anyone or cut spending out of fear.
  • Everyone thought alike. All five players turned to housing the moment a headline suggested it.
  • The referee made two small scoring errors, one generous and one harsh. A checker caught both and they were corrected the next round.
  • What this means for trusting the result. The direction is believable for a world this fast: fewer jobs, cheaper living, gains tilted to owners. The calm is not. With real layoffs the same losses would show up as higher unemployment and more anger, and politics would probably not have stayed this steady. The gain in living standards rests on homes being easy to build.
