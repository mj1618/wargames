A forgiving economy that absorbed very fast office AI.

The outcome in one sentence

Machines became able to do nine-tenths of desk work within ten years, yet by 2036 more adults had jobs than in 2026, pay was up by a third and housing was cheaper, although office work shrank steadily and business owners took a slowly growing share of the income.

What is this?

This is one of five play-throughs of the same fictional exercise, which asks what rapid progress in AI and robots would do to jobs and living standards in the United States between 2027 and 2036. AI agents played the federal government, the AI companies, large employers, households and entrepreneurs, each with its own goals and private information. A referee, dice and a documented economic model kept score, so this shows one way things could go and is not a forecast.

2

The hand this run was dealt

Before each play-through, dice set the underlying conditions of the economy. The players were never told what they were.

What was favourable

  • When things got cheaper or people got richer, they bought a lot more, which created work.
  • Many new kinds of human work appeared as old kinds were automated.
  • Pay rose along with what workers produced, so the gains were shared well.
  • Homes could be built quickly and cheaply wherever building was allowed.
  • Lost wages did not snowball into further job losses, because business investment filled most of the gap.
  • Companies took up AI at a moderate pace, and robots ran about five years behind office AI.
  • Washington could get a new programme running within a couple of years.

What was not

  • AI for desk work improved extremely fast. By 2036 machines could do 90% of it.
  • Companies passed on only part of their savings as lower prices and kept the rest as profit.
  • The big states refused to help with house building for the whole ten years.

The random events that came up

  • 2027A housing squeeze that pushed rents up.
  • 2028A share-market fall, after which banks cut back lending.
  • 2030A hit new industry that hired a lot of people.
  • 2031–32A shortage of electricity.
  • 2033–34A wave of mergers among the companies that supply AI.
  • 2035–36A court ruling that made companies fully liable for decisions made by their machines.
3

What happened

7 rounds, 10 years
  1. 2027

    Automation by not hiring

    Employers automated quietly. They did not replace people who left and they froze graduate hiring, so the damage showed up as missing first jobs, not as layoffs. Automation removed about 0.7 million jobs and unemployment barely moved, at 4.2%.

    Congress passed housing grants for cities that allow more building. A court blocked a rule that would have made companies report AI-related layoffs.

  2. 2028

    The one bad year, and machines did not cause it

    The share-market fall and the lending squeeze cut spending and destroyed 4.1 million jobs. Automation took another 0.7 million. Unemployment peaked at only 4.9%, because most people who lost work stopped looking and so were not counted.

    Employers and unions signed a deal that capped layoffs and shared profits with staff. In November the pro-growth government lost the election to a coalition that put workers first.

  3. 2029

    A sharp rebound and a lost bill

    Lending returned and 4.2 million jobs came back with it. Unemployment fell to 3.6%.

    The new government's main bill would have sent cheques to affected households, paid for by an extra 8-point tax on AI company profits. The referee gave it a 70% chance. The dice fell well outside that and it failed. A smaller bill passed: companies must now report layoffs, and one million public jobs would start in 2030. The AI companies opened a cheap service for small firms.

  4. 2030

    Two lucky arrivals at once

    The hit new industry and the public jobs arrived in the same year. Automation removed 1.3 million jobs and 3.3 million were created, and unemployment fell to 3.3%.

    A smaller, 5-point tax on AI profits passed, also at 70% odds. It paid for wage insurance, which tops up the pay of people who move to a lower-paid job, and for a first-job programme for young people.

  5. 2031–32

    A power shortage slows the machines

    The electricity shortage slowed the take-up of AI just as startups made their biggest hiring push. Automation removed 2.6 million jobs and 3.9 million were created. The government's competition cases against the AI companies were settled with binding rules that gave other firms access to computing power.

  6. 2033–34

    Automation doubles

    Automation removed 5.1 million jobs, twice the previous pace, and half of them were office jobs. Only 4.7 million were created. This was the one period after 2028 when fewer jobs were created than destroyed. Employment still rose, because the population grew.

    A law opening up access to computing power passed at half its intended size. That was just enough to stop the merger wave from pushing up the price of AI.

  7. 2035–36

    Caution returns, and so does job growth

    The liability ruling made AI companies and employers more careful, and automation slowed to 4.3 million jobs. New jobs reached 5.1 million, so creation overtook destruction again.

Did new jobs outrun lost jobs here?

Yes, for the economy as a whole. No, for office workers. About 19.8 million jobs were destroyed over the ten years: 15.7 million by automation and 4.1 million by the 2028 slump. About 22.9 million were created. Of those, 12.3 million were new kinds of work, 8.8 million came from people having more to spend (nearly half of that was simply the 2029 rebound), and 1.0 million were public jobs. Employment rose from 162.4 million to 172.2 million, helped also by a growing population.

The idea being tested is that when machines make something cheaper, people buy so much more of it that the trade ends up employing more people, not fewer. That held only partly here. Employers kept about two-thirds of their savings as profit, so prices barely fell: goods were 1.5% cheaper after ten years. Almost no jobs came from cheaper prices. People were re-employed through new kinds of work and higher incomes instead.

Office workers as a group did not get their jobs back. Office employment fell from 48.0 million to 44.8 million, while care, building, retail and the new industries all grew. The average working week also shortened, from 34.3 to 32.7 hours.

6

Did ordinary people end up better off here?

Yes, broadly. After allowing for prices, the typical household's income rose 27% and the poorest fifth's rose 22%. Housing costs fell 17.5%. The share of people in poverty fell from 12.9% to 8.7%, and homelessness fell from 22 to 14 per 10,000 people.

Who lost out. Office workers and new graduates, who met a hiring freeze in 2027 and 2028. Everyone who lost work in the 2028 slump, when 3.75 million fewer people had jobs within one year. The poorest fifth gained less than the middle. Wages' share of national income fell from 52.8% to 50.4% and the top 1% share rose from 20.0% to 20.9%, so owners gained faster than workers. Health care became slightly dearer. Government debt rose from 100% to about 110% of the size of the economy, although the yearly deficit narrowed.

7

What drove it

Mostly the hand that was dealt. Plentiful new kinds of work, easy house building and pay that kept up with productivity did the heavy lifting.

Three choices by the players mattered. Employers chose to shrink through people leaving and through shorter weeks, not layoffs. The AI companies' cheap service for small firms removed a brake on startups. Washington's 2027 housing grants let the easy building happen even though the big states would not help.

The dice shaped the timing. They produced the 2028 slump. They killed the main cheques-and-tax bill of 2029 despite its 70% chance, so help arrived later and smaller. They let the computing-access law through at half size. The referee had given that law an 80% chance of passing. An independent checker argued that 60% was the right figure, and at 60% the same roll would have failed. Had it failed, the mergers would have raised the price of AI.

8

Where the players were unrealistic

The independent checker twice described the players as individually plausible but too cosy as a group. Nobody broke ranks in ten years.

  • Employers were generous without being forced. They kept the layoff cap, the profit-sharing pool and a minimum level of entry-level hiring, even though the unions' recruitment drive failed in almost every round and so put little pressure on them.
  • The AI companies volunteered money. They paid about $5 billion a year into a fund for displaced workers without being asked, and proposed a tax on their own profits.
  • Households never used their strongest card. They did not strike when workers were scarce and their bargaining power was at its highest.

What this means for trusting the result: real employers and AI companies would probably have cut harder and shared less. The job numbers rest mainly on the dealt conditions, so they would likely survive tougher players. The fairly even sharing of the gains leans more on the players' good behaviour, so treat it as the optimistic end of what this hand could produce.
