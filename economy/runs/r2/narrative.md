# Run r2 — summary

## 1. This play-through in three sentences

Machines came to do 61% of desk work and 38% of physical work, and only 0.84 new jobs appeared per job removed, so the share of adults in work fell from 59.1% to 56.1%. Unemployment hardly moved, because most people who lost work stopped looking. Households still ended about a fifth better off, thanks to cheap housing and higher hourly pay, while workers' share of national income fell from 52.8% to 38.8%.

## 2. The hidden conditions and the events

The players were dealt a hard jobs economy with a soft floor under living costs. Desk-work AI was at the fastest end, robots only about three years behind, and firms adopted quickly. Cheaper things drew in only moderately more buying, a middling amount of new human work appeared, savings were split between prices and profit, pay did not share in the gains, and lost wages snowballed into lost sales. On the kind side: homes were easy to build, big states were already overriding zoning, the central bank was willing to ease, and Washington could legislate under a growth-first coalition.

Events drawn by dice: new jobs proving automatable (2027), a leap in AI capability (2028), a boom in cheap AI services (2029), a financial wobble (2030), a data revision (2031–32), a land squeeze in boom cities (2033–34) and a successful state wage-insurance pilot (2035–36).

## 3. The story

- **2027–28.** The AI labs race and employers automate through hiring freezes. Employment falls 1.5m.
- **2029.** The one easy year. The services boom and a founders' hiring push create 6.2m jobs against 3.7m lost.
- **2030.** AI shares fall by a third. 9.0m jobs go, 5.3m of them because spending fell, not to machines. Employment drops 5.1m.
- **2031–32.** The central bank eases and spending returns: 13.5m jobs created against 9.9m lost. A cash dividend for the bottom two-fifths fails in Congress.
- **2033–34.** Robots scale. 13.7m jobs destroyed, a record. Renewal of a small levy on AI profits fails.
- **2035–36.** Another 11.0m jobs go and 8.6m appear. A half-size levy and state wage-insurance match pass.

## 4. Answer 1 — jobs

Over ten years 53.5m jobs were destroyed and 44.9m created. About 22.7m of the new jobs were new kinds of work, 13.3m came from cheaper output selling more, and 7.5m were spending recovering after slumps. Employment ended at 160.3m, 2.1m below 2026 despite a larger population. Unemployment went from 4.1% to 4.3%, peaking at 4.5% in 2030; participation fell from 61.6% to 58.6%, and the working week shrank from 34.3 to 31.5 hours.

A Jevons effect did not hold for the economy as a whole. It held in care (38.0m to 41.0m), building and new industries (4.6m to 11.7m). It failed in offices (48.0m to 39.1m), factories and logistics. Capability and adoption were so fast, and robots so close behind, that a middling supply of new work could not keep up.

## 5. Answer 2 — living standards

Median household income reached 120.6 (2026 = 100; the no-AI trend gives about 109). The bottom fifth reached 118.7. The top 1% share rose from 20% to 25.8%. Housing costs fell 24% and professional services 12%; goods fell 7%; health barely moved. Poverty fell from 12.9% to 9.2%, homelessness from 22 to 13 per 10,000. The deficit eased from 6.0% to 5.3% of GDP, but revenue slid from 17.3% to 16.0% as the wage tax base shrank.

## 6. What drove it

Mostly the hidden conditions: fast machines set the job losses, easy housing the gain in living standards. Three decisions mattered. The labs raced in every round, so machines could do 90% of desk work by 2034. Employers chose attrition, shorter weeks and profit-sharing, which hid the losses from the unemployment rate and lifted hourly pay. Washington passed four housing bills but never a meaningful cash transfer or tax on AI profits. Dice set the 2030 slump and kept the growth-first coalition in office through four elections, each with roughly a one-in-three chance of losing.

## 7. Where the players were unrealistic

The corporate players were too polite. While workers' share fell 14 points, employers never ran mass layoffs and labs offered to be taxed. Households never struck, boycotted or panicked. All five pivoted to housing the moment a headline suggested it.
