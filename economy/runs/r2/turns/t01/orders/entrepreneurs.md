# Orders — Entrepreneurs — Turn 01

## Read of the situation

We're seeing real customer demand emerging for new kinds of services—about one-third of our network reports customer needs they couldn't have named two years ago, mostly small orders from households and firms wanting bespoke work. The growth-first coalition runs Washington and several states are already loosening zoning, which opens housing and care opportunities. Credit is cheap and available, but the "AI supervisor" play of 2026 is imploding as those roles get automated, which tells us the demand trap: hiring for automation doesn't build sustainable businesses.

## Options considered

1. **Double down on AI-only firms**: hire nobody, maximize margins, move fast—profitable per founder but we cede "job creator" narrative and won't reach the household and craft demand showing up.
2. **Play it safe**: expand slowly in proven niches, hire as-needed, wait for the VC cycle—conservative but misses the window while states are loosening zoning.
3. **Build the infrastructure play**: founders act as a hub; coordinate factory-built housing with AI-assisted construction, licensing support, and financing—takes coordination but zoning tailwinds are real now.

## Decisions this period

1. **Pivot to customer-led growth**: shift capital from speculative AI-only ventures to people-heavy services (care, trades, bespoke work, experiences) where we're seeing actual demand. New business wave at +15% above baseline (balanced mix, not all AI-only). Reason: the 2026 supervisor disaster shows automation for its own sake fails; real customers are buying genuine new services.

2. **Lean into housing and zoning**: launch a coordinated push into factory-built housing in states already loosening zoning (factory techniques + robot-assisted assembly + permitting help). Invest $50M+ across the network into pilot projects. Reason: zoning tailwinds won't last, and housing is the bottleneck states are actually trying to solve.

3. **Political play**: advocate hard for antitrust against AI giants (pricing/access), easier licensing for new service categories, and credit guarantees for small firms in capital-intensive sectors. Reason: our survival depends on not getting locked in to giant firms' terms and on customers having income to spend.

## Public statement

**"The demand we're seeing is real and it's for people and expertise, not just automation for its own sake. We're moving capital into services where customers actually want human judgment and craft, and we're going to build housing wherever zoning will let us."**

## Messages

**To: Workers**
*Text: "We're hiring—especially in care, trades and bespoke services. If you can do something a customer will pay for and bigger firms haven't commodified yet, we want to talk."*

**To: Consumers**
*Text: "New kinds of businesses are opening up this year. If there's something you've wanted but couldn't find (care, repairs, advice, building projects) there's probably a founder working on it now."*

## What would change my mind

If credit suddenly dries up or customer spending collapses, we'd cut the new-business push fast and focus on cash-positive operations.

## Journal entry

We're betting on real demand for services and expertise over automation theater. The 2026 "AI supervisor" crash showed that hiring people just to manage machines doesn't work—customers want the work *done*, not supervised. We're shifting capital from AI-only specs to people-heavy services where we're actually seeing traction. Factory-built housing is the play: states are loosening zoning right now, construction is a labor bottleneck AI can genuinely help with, and every house built creates supply-side relief for the whole economy. We'll watch whether credit stays cheap and whether states follow through on zoning. If both hold, we scale fast.
