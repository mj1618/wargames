# Run r3 — summary

## 1. This play-through in three sentences

Machines became able to do 90% of desk work within ten years, yet by 2036 more adults had jobs than in 2026, pay was up a third and rents were down. The only bad year was 2028, and that was a financial wobble, not automation. The gains were broad, but office work shrank steadily and owners of capital took a slowly growing share.

## 2. The hidden conditions and the events

The players did not know they had been dealt a forgiving economy. Desk-work AI improved extremely fast, but firms adopted it at a moderate pace and robots ran about five years behind. Cheaper things pulled in a lot of extra demand, many new kinds of human work appeared, pay shared well in productivity gains, homes could be built almost freely, and lost wages did not snowball. Washington could act within a couple of years. The big states refused to help with housing throughout.

Events drawn by dice: a housing squeeze (2027), a share-market fall and credit squeeze (2028), a hit new industry (2030), a power shortage (2031–32), a merger wave among AI providers (2033–34) and a court ruling making firms fully liable for automated decisions (2035–36).

## 3. The story

- **2027.** Employers automate by not replacing leavers and freezing graduate hiring. Congress passes housing grants for cities that allow building. A court blocks the layoff-disclosure rule.
- **2028.** The credit squeeze destroys 4.1m jobs; automation only 0.7m. Unemployment peaks at 4.9% because most who lost work stopped looking. Employers and unions sign a layoff cap and profit-sharing deal. The pro-growth coalition loses the election to a worker-first one.
- **2029.** Credit returns and 4.2m jobs come back. The government's flagship bill (cheques funded by an 8-point tax on AI profits) had a 70% chance and failed. A smaller bill passes: layoff reporting and 1m public jobs. The AI labs open a cheap tier for small firms.
- **2030.** The new industry and the public jobs arrive together. A 5-point AI-profit levy passes, funding wage insurance and a first-job programme.
- **2031–32.** The power shortage slows adoption; startups hire heavily. Antitrust cases settle with binding rules on access to computing power.
- **2033–34.** Automation doubles to 5.1m jobs, the one period after 2028 when fewer jobs were created than destroyed. A law opening access to computing power passes at half size, just enough to stop the mergers raising prices.
- **2035–36.** The liability ruling makes labs and employers cautious. Creation overtakes destruction again.

## 4. Answer 1 — jobs

About 19.8m jobs were destroyed over ten years (15.7m by automation, 4.1m by the 2028 slump) and 22.9m created. Of those, 12.3m were new kinds of work, 8.8m came from spending (half of it simply the 2029 rebound), and 1.0m were public jobs. Population growth added more. Employment rose from 162.4m to 172.2m. Unemployment went 4.1% → 4.9% (2028) → 3.3%; participation 61.6% → 60.3% → 62.2%. Office jobs fell from 48.0m to 44.8m; care, building, retail and the new sectors grew. The working week shortened from 34.3 to 32.7 hours.

The Jevons effect (cheaper output creating more work in the same trade) held only partly. Employers kept about two-thirds of their savings, so prices barely fell and almost no jobs came from cheaper prices. The economy as a whole re-employed people through new work and higher incomes; office workers as a group did not get their jobs back.

## 5. Answer 2 — living standards

Median household real income rose 27%, the bottom fifth 22%. The top 1% share went from 20.0% to 20.9%, and labour's share of income fell from 52.8% to 50.4%. Housing costs fell 17.5%. Goods fell 1.5%, digital services were flat, health care rose 1.4%. Poverty fell from 12.9% to 8.7%; homelessness from 22 to 14 per 10,000. The deficit narrowed from 6.0% to 5.2% of GDP; debt rose from 100% to 109.5%.

## 6. What drove it

Mostly the hidden conditions: plentiful new work, easy building and well-shared pay did the heavy lifting. Three decisions mattered. Employers chose attrition and shorter weeks over layoffs. The labs' cheap small-firm tier removed a brake on startups. Washington's 2027 housing law let the easy building happen despite the states. Dice shaped the timing: the 2028 slump, the failed 70% bill, and the half-passed access law. The referee's auditor noted that under a stricter reading that last bill would have failed, and AI prices would have risen.

## 7. Where the players were unrealistic

The auditor twice called them "collectively cosy". Employers kept a layoff cap, profit pool and entry-hiring floor although the unions' organising drive failed in five rounds of seven. The labs paid $5bn a year unasked and volunteered a levy. Households never struck at peak leverage. Nobody defected in ten years.
