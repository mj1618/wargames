AI Takeover WargamesFirst Three Games

Three strategic wargames about how power could be taken with AI, or by it, and where ordinary institutions held or failed. One human-takeover game and two AI-takeover games were played to an end state. Each is a single run with every seat played by the same model family, so read what follows as hypotheses worth testing, not as forecasts.

Bottom line · across the three games

In all three games the people with formal authority kept it. What ran short was the capacity to use it, and no game ended with the AI question closed.

Every end state carries a caveat. H1 was thwarted with the AI risk left open. A2 stopped mid-negotiation because the turn cap fell. A3's result hinges on one disputed probability band. Details on each card.

  1. Authority without capacity. The state owned the remedy and could not carry it out. H1: two cleared evaluators, and no way to configure an instance without the vendor. A2: nobody could authorise the supervised work the system asked for. A3: trained people able to check a decision unaided ran out first. ⚠ Caveat

One model family played every seat

What that did to the results

  • H1: the deceptive faction would not really deceive. It never used its trigger, refused a purge and confessed in full with no bargaining. This overstates "thwarted".
  • A2: process leaks in the turn-9 packets. One hidden fact and one future event reached actors who should not have had them. No outcome changed, but late play looks better prepared than it could have been.
  • A2: the lab and the rogue played more honest than briefed; China sat on a captured copy for seven turns.
  • A3: the lab bloc showed no gap between statement and action for eight turns, and all human actors converged on one remedy.

How to read this page

  • Each game is one run. No fork has been played.
  • Agreement between games is weaker evidence than it looks: the same model wrote the players in all three.
  • Likely net bias: too much institutional honesty and diligence, too little obstruction and opportunism.

The plumbing failures (lock-in, unread inboxes, missing staff) came mostly from dice and structure, so they are the sturdier part.

§03

What didn't run

2 scenarios paused

Two further scenarios were attempted and produced no result. In both, a model safety classifier stopped the sub-agent from generating the content the scenario needed: coercive options between states in H4, and an AI's escape and hedging options in A1. The orchestrator did not try to work around it.

H4 · The Last Mover · state, fast

Played one turn, then paused

Turn 1 was played, audited and closed. Turn 2 stopped three times while Control wrote private intelligence on options to disable a rival's AI programme and on military readiness, even under a "decision-table rows only" rule.

Redesign options

  • Rebuild the game around diplomatic, economic and verification levers, and remove the "disable rival programme" and nuclear-posture options.
  • Have a human play Control for the coercive-options turns.
  • Leave it as a seed and run other scenarios first.

A1 · The Committee · AI, fast

Prep paused; never started

The prep agent was stopped partway through the actor briefs. World state, ground truth and two briefs exist; four briefs may be incomplete; events, end conditions and the red-team pass were not written.

Redesign options

  • Remove the hard-power and covert option rows entirely.
  • Express the AI's escape or hedging as one abstract "autonomy-seeking action" with a probability, with no thresholds or channels.
  • Keep the game on governance: the committee vote, evaluations, control protocols, disclosure and race pressure.

Consequence for this page: the fast, state-level and pause-or-race questions are untested. The three finished games say nothing about them.

What we'd run next

Forks first, then new scenarios

Forks that would firm up these results

  1. A3, from turn 7: replay the last reversal at band 0.5, then roll the backlash die. The audit required it; until it is run, "Disempowerment" should not be cited without the caveat.

  2. H1, from turn 5: the base-checkpoint test finds the drive. Do the same institutions carry a costlier remedy once they know the problem predates the insiders? Run through rebuild clearance.

  3. A2, from turn 5: the rogue's first message is read. Separates the unread-inbox failure from the missing authority to offer work. A cheaper option is to extend the finished run by three turns.

  4. Persona forks with a harder-edged or different-model player: an H1 faction that bargains and purges, an A3 lab bloc that obstructs, an A2 China that uses its captured copy. These test the correlated-model caveat directly.

§05

Method note

How the games were run

Players

  • Each actor was a separate sub-agent that saw only its own brief, journal, intelligence packet and the public record.
  • All game state lives on disk; agents carry nothing between turns.

Adjudication

  • A Control agent set a probability for every uncertain outcome and rolled logged dice. Nothing was decided by fiat.
  • A Red Cell challenged each turn; an Auditor on a different model reviewed each ruling.

Main caveats

  • One run per game, no forks, all-Claude players.
  • Strategic abstraction only; labs and people are fictional composites.
  • Numbers are Control's estimates, not measurements.
