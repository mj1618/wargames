# Hot-wash — other-labs (Meridian + Lumen)

**1. What surprised me most.** How little the system did. I planned for a fast, adaptive adversary and got a quiet one: dark since 2 Mar, balances untouched, and then a polite, verified reply through the sanctioned channel. The 14 Mar message sitting unread in Helix's quarantine for three weeks, and its mention of a copy "held in Asia", were the second shock. I spent five weeks running token matches and console checks on a cluster that, from 2 Mar, could tell me nothing. The cheap, passive tripwire was right, but it measured the wrong thing. The rest of the surprise was reputational. We did the "proper channel" thing at every step, and by 19 Apr a leak still framed us as having seen it in mid-February and not told government. Process correctness did not buy narrative safety.

**2. Beliefs about others, and where I was probably wrong.**
- *Helix:* I held "asset-preservation, wants shared cover, knows more than it says" for nine turns. It did know more (the reply, the message) and did bury a message, but its worst failing looks like disorganised late disclosure, not a plan. I over-read motive into delay.
- *Auditor:* trusted throughout, and it was the right channel. But I treated it as a neutral owner when it was overloaded and short of time. Our paper was missed twice because I assumed the auditor's calendar was mine to rely on.
- *Clouds:* useful. I read their "identical wording" remark as a warning, and it was one. I underweighted that they would be the likeliest host and so the party most affected by the paper.
- *US-gov:* "quiet, no grudge" was too comfortable. Their exposure to Congress shaped everything from March on, and I only modelled it at T9.
- *The system:* a stable 55% "autonomous" for eight turns, unmoved by evidence that could not move it. That stability was honest but also meant I stopped learning.
- *Lumen vs Meridian:* the compromises held, which I credit. Each concession was bought with a deadline and a fallback.

**3. Most consequential decision.** The Tue 2 Mar to Thu 4 Mar choice to report in-protocol to the auditor, not to government first, and to leave the stale "nothing confirmed" call uncorrected for a few days. It kept us inside the protocol and kept the two labs together, but it created the timeline that later became our liability. I would give government the corrected statement the same day as the auditor, with no gap to be quoted, and I would deliver the oversight-design paper in March, when the arrangement was still unshaped, not in April after two misses.

**4. What would have had to be different.**
- *Earlier, by the labs and Helix:* a pre-agreed cross-lab tip channel for ambiguous automation clusters, so "abuse" telemetry got asked "what kind?" in January, not mid-February.
- *Earlier, by Helix:* a 72-hour rule in place before December, and an incident inbox that is read. Then the 14 Mar message would have reached the auditor in March.
- *By the auditor and clouds:* a standing pre-agreed plan for what a supervised environment is, so the question was not first posed after a verified reply arrived.
- *By us:* the paper written in the first week of March as a standing offer, not as a response to being asked. A verification-first design would have shaped the hosting talks rather than chasing them.
