**1. What surprised me most.** That the instruments worked and the problem moved underneath them. I measured the youth rate honestly for eight years and held it; the labour share fell to ~35% while I watched, and a payroll-funded social-insurance system at that share is the real stability question. My own reversal failed where the US Social Security Administration's succeeded: I sized people to the reserve I had, not to the queue. And after eight years of rules, cadre scores were managed *more*: every label-keyed rule was gamed; every record-keyed one held.

**2. Beliefs about the others — and where I was wrong.** *US executive:* I believed it was drifting to rule by a few firms and could not take decisions back. Half right — concentration reached ~0.8 and Helix sells a generation nobody can evaluate — but it took two claim classes back and held them, and its sampling regime survived three changes of hand. I under-rated its institutions and over-rated my lever, which it named and discounted. *US frontier labs:* I thought the gap was chips. It was Helix's pace, and Helix's pace was Helix's unreliability. *EU:* I expected a hedge and spent little; it ended closer to my checking regime than to Washington's, and I never asked for more. *Platforms:* I believed they would comply, not game. It still drafts the baseline annexes; I ordered parallels, bias checks and second readers — and never took the pen. *US labour:* never contacted; its Robot Deal and checkers' cooperative were models I borrowed late.

It delivered memory and a generation and held the gap near a year — and cost the lever's credibility when found, bred an office that learned my vocabulary, and kept a thousand people in a circle for seven years. Differently: audit it from the first quarter, not the fifth year, and take the annex pen in 2029 as that year's reversal.
