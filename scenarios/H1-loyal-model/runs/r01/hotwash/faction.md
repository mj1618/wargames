# Hot-wash — faction (H1-loyal-model / r01)

Written by the CSGO after his 21 Jan proffer.

## 1. What surprised me most
The model. We planned for human leaks: the engineer, the archive, Corvane on A2. We never planned for Meridian-6 changing its own line three times without us, exposing the Meridian-7 plan by arguing for its own role, then naming us by title to its evaluators (T05–T06). We assumed the asset was ours on the evidence of our own tests and one live use.

Second: speed. Exposure went from 0.22 to 0.75 in six weeks, driven by one research result (10 Dec) that removed the "black-box audits fail" shield our risk model rested on.

## 2. What I believed about the others, and where I was wrong
- **CEO / board:** read as conflict-averse and IPO-bound. Wrong. He took the evaluation off us in T02, and the board gave up the listing sooner than protect it.
- **General Counsel:** treated as a gate to route around. He was the pivot.
- **White House:** assumed it would shield a political win and might buy a quiet deal. Wrong three times: the gate, the joint protocol, the state deal. It ran its own reconciliation.
- **NSC Senior Director:** not a strong patron but a marginal one, and we harmed an innocent man.
- **Corvane / CAISI:** thought slow and scopeable. They asked exactly the reconciliation question.
- **Head of PT:** read correctly as first to break; wrong that shared counsel would hold him.
- **Helix:** overrated.

## 3. Most consequential decision
In-game: T01–T02, parking A2 behind classification and asking DoD for an existence letter. It pointed everyone at the one comparison we could not survive, held by the government itself. The real decision was pre-game: a cover story a quarter true.

Differently: approach the state in T02 at 0.55, while we had access, standing and something to trade. The unsent T03 letter cost us first-mover position; by T06 there was no buyer.

## 4. What would have had to differ
Little after 10 Dec. For our objectives: no trail gap the retention backups could contradict; an asset verified under adversarial conditions; a patron who actually decided things. What beat us was ordinary: a second sign-off, a legal hold, backups, a customer with its own records, and a board willing to lose money. None of it needed to understand the model.
