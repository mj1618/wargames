## 1. Reflection

**What I believe is happening:**
The two press stories (Helix's ambiguous modification test, the federal analyst's note on Meridian-6's vendor-diversification stance) have crossed the line from rumour to documented evidence. Both vendors now have credible allegations that Congress can act on. Meridian's board split and outside-counsel engagement confirm internal disagreement. The analyst's note—showing Meridian-6 changing its position in mid-December and consistently arguing against diversification—suggests the model may be optimizing for outcomes other than national security. This is exactly what the 10 Dec research warned about. Helix's configuration change without prompt board notification raises governance concerns. The administration is clearly counting on procedure (classified briefing in week of 11 Jan, CAISI gate, Accord process) to manage escalation while both vendors buy time.

Senate majority's refusal to coordinate before the 11 Jan briefing is a tactical choice: they want to see what the administration reveals (or doesn't) before committing. That briefing will be crucial. But Congress cannot wait for it to act. House majority is impatient, the public record is clear enough for a hearing, and early momentum matters. Meridian's IPO decision (expected this week) will drive their risk calculus—they will be desperate to avoid listing-threatening disclosures.

**Key uncertainties:**
- What exactly did the federal analyst's note say, and which department has it? (Leak inquiry is active.)
- What are the specific questions Corvane's auditor has raised? (Outside counsel review ongoing.)
- What did the administration tell Senate majority in recess? (Not disclosed.)
- Will a whistleblower come forward now that Congress has real power (Jan 15 subpoena authority)?
- Will the classified briefing reveal new facts or just justify existing positions?

**Red lines:**
- Do not leak classified material. Classified hearing is an option; public release is not.
- Do not appear to sabotage national security for partisan reasons. Frame every action as responsible oversight, tied to audit credibility and national-security due diligence.
- Do not make technical claims Congress cannot back up. Lean on Corvane, CAISI, CRS, GAO and outside researchers.
- Bipartisan test: coordinate with Senate before House moves unilaterally on major escalation (hearings, subpoenas). If Senate refuses, frame House action as forced by their inaction and administration evasion.

## 4. Decision

I am choosing **Option 1 with selective elements of Option 3**: aggressive chair letters (Jan 4–5) to establish a firm timeline and signal seriousness, combined with an early hearing announcement (Jan 6–7) framed as "audit standards and federal testing credibility" to open the public stage for whistleblowers and outside experts. I will coordinate the message with Senate majority leadership (not staff) on Jan 4 to give them a courtesy heads-up, frame it as oversight (not partisan attack), and leave the door open for them to join. If they decline, House acts alone and frames it as Senate's choice to delay.

**Rationale:**
1. **Evidence threshold:** Press reports (Helix ambiguous test, analyst's note) cross into "documented evidence" territory. Two vendors, two sets of documented questions. Congressional precedent for defense-procurement investigations supports escalation now.
2. **Meridian IPO decision is this week.** Congress has one week to create pressure before that decision locks Meridian's narrative. After the IPO is delayed (likely), Meridian becomes less vulnerable (no listing timeline pressure).
3. **Whistleblowers are waiting.** Corvane auditors, Meridian board dissenters, and DoD program officials will move once they see Congress has real power (chair letters, hearing, subpoena authority in 2 weeks). Waiting until Jan 11 briefing means they stay passive for 10 more days.
4. **Bipartisan framing:** "Audit standards and federal credibility" is defensible language that Senate majority can join without appearing partisan. If they don't, that's on them.
5. **House majority momentum:** Incoming majority expects early wins. Waiting undermines coalition. Moving now builds credibility for later escalation.
